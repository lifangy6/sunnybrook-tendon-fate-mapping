## 1. Start Here: The Clinical Problem

Imagine tearing your Achilles tendon — the thick cord at the back of your ankle — or a rotator cuff tendon in your shoulder. Millions of people do every year. Surgeons can stitch the torn ends back together, but the healed tissue is never the same. It becomes a dense, disorganized scar that is permanently weaker, stiffer, and far more prone to re-injury than the original tendon. Professional athletes are told their career may be over. Ordinary people lose the ability to climb stairs without pain.

The central question driving this research is: **why can't adult tendons regenerate properly, and what would it take to make them do so?**

This is not purely an academic question. Rotator cuff tears alone affect roughly 17% of the general population and cost the US healthcare system billions of dollars annually. The failure rate of surgical repairs is startlingly high — up to 40% re-tear within two years, and the mechanical properties of even a "successful" repair never return to baseline.

To fix this, we need to understand, at the level of individual cells and genes, exactly what happens inside a tendon after injury — and why that process goes wrong in adults.

## 2. What Is a Tendon, and Why Is It So Hard to Fix?

### Tendons as engineering marvels

A tendon is the cable that connects a muscle to a bone. When your calf muscle contracts, it yanks on the Achilles tendon, which pulls your heel up and propels you forward. Tendons must transmit enormous forces — the Achilles tendon routinely bears loads of 7–10 times body weight during running — while remaining flexible enough to bend around corners.

How does a tissue achieve this? The answer lies in its microscopic structure: **type I collagen**, the same protein in leather and rope, organized into thick parallel bundles running the entire length of the tendon. Imagine thousands of cables twisted together, all perfectly aligned in one direction. Between these collagen cables sit specialized cells called **tenocytes** — long, thin cells that look almost like stretched-out spindles. Their job is to continuously produce and maintain the collagen matrix that surrounds them.

This highly ordered structure is what gives tendons their remarkable strength. And it is precisely this order that is almost impossible to recreate once it is disrupted.

### The healing problem

When a tendon is torn, the body's default emergency response kicks in: plug the hole with scar tissue. Scar is essentially a rushed, improvised repair — a tangle of disorganized collagen thrown in quickly to restore structural continuity. It is strong enough to prevent the tissue from falling apart, but it lacks the beautiful parallel organization of native tendon. Under the microscope, a healed tendon looks like scrambled eggs where it should look like strands of spaghetti lying flat. Under load, this disorganized structure fails at a fraction of the force a normal tendon would withstand.

The root cause of this failure is **cellular**: the right cells — the ones that can rebuild organized tendon — do not show up in sufficient numbers at the injury site in adults. Understanding *which cells those are*, *where they come from*, and *why they fail to act* is the core of this research.

## 3. The Central Mystery: Babies Heal, Adults Scar

One of the most striking findings in this field is that **newborn mice can fully regenerate tendons that adults cannot**.

If you surgically remove a full-thickness section of patellar tendon (the tendon connecting the kneecap to the shin bone) in a neonatal mouse, the animal grows back a new, properly organized tendon — a "neo-tendon" — within weeks. Gait returns to normal. Mechanical properties are restored. It is, by every measure, true regeneration.

Do the exact same injury in an adult mouse, and you get scar. Dense, disorganized fibrous tissue fills the defect. The animal walks with a permanent limp. The mechanical properties never recover.

This is not unique to mice; the same age-dependent loss of regenerative capacity is observed across many tissues and species, including humans. Fetal wounds heal without scars; adult wounds do not.

**Why?** The answer appears to lie not just in the *environment* of the tissue (the signals available), but in the *cells themselves*. Transplanting neonatal mesenchymal cells (the stem-cell-like progenitors described later) into injured adult tendons can partially restore regenerative healing — suggesting that something about the cellular state of young progenitors is fundamentally different from that of adult ones.

Unlocking that difference is a major goal of this research program.

## 4. Cells: The Actors in the Story

Before going further, let's introduce the main cellular players. Think of the tissue as a theater, and these are the characters.

### The stage: connective tissue

All the cells below live in **connective tissue** — the structural scaffolding of the body. Connective tissue includes tendons, ligaments, cartilage, bone, the walls of blood vessels, and the loose fibrous tissue that wraps and separates muscles. It is made mostly of collagen and other proteins secreted by cells.

### Tenocytes — the tendon workers

Tenocytes are the resident cells of the tendon midsubstance. Their entire existence is devoted to maintaining the collagen matrix that gives the tendon its strength. They are identified by the molecular "address label" **Scleraxis (Scx)** — a protein (technically a transcription factor) that tenocytes produce and that is used in experiments to mark them with colored tags.

Think of tenocytes as the tendon's maintenance crew. When the tendon is healthy, they go about their business quietly. After injury, adult tenocytes are notably reluctant to migrate into the wound — one of the central reasons adult tendons heal poorly.

### Tendon Stem/Progenitor Cells (TSPCs) — the regenerators

Hidden in the outer sheath of the tendon (the **paratenon**) lives a small population of stem-cell-like cells marked by a protein called **Tppp3**. These cells are mostly dormant in the healthy adult tendon, dividing rarely. But after a particular type of tendon injury (a punch-biopsy wound of the patellar tendon, for example), they wake up, multiply, migrate into the defect, and produce new tenocytes.

A subset of these Tppp3+ cells that also expresses a surface receptor called **PDGFRα** (Tppp3+PDGFRα+) has been shown to be the true regenerative unit — the ones with genuine stem cell function. Remove their PDGFRα and the tendon fails to regenerate.

Importantly, not every tendon injury activates these cells equally — which is why *how* you injure the tendon in an experiment matters as much as *which* tendon you injure.

### T-FAPs — the scar-formers

Living in the same sheath as the TSPCs is a completely different cell type: **T-FAPs** (Tendon Fibroadipogenic Progenitors). They also express PDGFRα but do *not* express Tppp3. This seemingly small difference has enormous consequences: after injury, T-FAPs do not become new tenocytes — they become **scar cells**, depositing disorganized collagen and driving fibrosis.

The same signal (PDGF, from the receptor PDGFRα) simultaneously tells TSPCs to regenerate and tells T-FAPs to scar. This dual-edged signaling is one reason that simply "turning up" a pro-healing signal is unlikely to work as a therapy — you might get more scar alongside more regeneration.

### Fibroadipogenic Progenitors (FAPs) — the orchestrators

FAPs are a broader family of stem-cell-like progenitors found in the interstitial spaces throughout connective tissue — not just in tendon, but in muscle, fat, heart, and skin. Their name reflects their capacity to become either fibroblasts (cells that make connective tissue) or adipocytes (fat cells), depending on the signals they receive.

In skeletal muscle, FAPs are essential for coordinating regeneration after injury: they clear cellular debris, secrete growth factors that support muscle fiber repair, and deposit provisional matrix. But if FAP activity is not properly controlled, they produce excess scar or fat deposits instead of functional tissue — a pathological outcome seen in diseases like muscular dystrophy.

FAPs can be divided into two major subtypes:
- **FAP1**: structural cells, specialized in building the extracellular matrix (the fibrous scaffolding)
- **FAP2**: signaling cells, specialized in communicating with other cell types via secreted molecules

### Mesenchymal Progenitors (MPs) — the master class

Zooming out, all of the above (TSPCs, T-FAPs, FAPs, and more) belong to a larger family: **Mesenchymal Progenitors**. "Mesenchymal" refers to their embryonic origin in a tissue called mesenchyme, from which all connective tissues derive. "Progenitor" means they can give rise to multiple specialized cell types.

Mesenchymal progenitors are the hub characters of this research. In the adult body, they sit quietly in tissues throughout the body — around blood vessels, in the spaces between fibers — waiting. When injury occurs, they activate. What they then become — scar cell, fat cell, tenocyte, bone cell — depends on their subtype, their location, and the molecular signals they receive. Understanding and controlling that decision is the therapeutic goal.

### Myofibroblasts — the scar drivers

Myofibroblasts are activated fibroblasts that express a protein called **alpha-smooth muscle actin (αSMA)**. They are the primary scar-forming cells after tendon injury. Experiments show that after rotator cuff repair in adult mice, the repaired region is dominated by αSMA-expressing (SMA-lineage) cells, while the original tenocytes (Scx-lineage cells) are largely absent. The scar is not a neutral placeholder — it actively takes over the healing site.

## 5. Fate Mapping: Watching Cells and Their Children

A central challenge in this field is tracking cells over time, across injury and repair. If you see a red-colored cell in a healed tendon, where did it come from? Was it a tenocyte that survived the injury? A progenitor that migrated in from the sheath? A fibroblast that came from a distant tissue?

**Fate mapping** is the experimental strategy that answers these questions definitively. The core idea is to permanently mark a cell with a heritable label — like tattooing it — so that it and *all its descendants* carry that mark forever, regardless of what they differentiate into later.

### The Cre-lox genetic system

The most powerful tool for fate mapping in mice is the **Cre-lox system**. Here is how it works:

1. **The reporter**: A mouse is engineered with a gene for a red fluorescent protein (tdTomato) in its genome. However, this gene has a "STOP sign" in front of it — a short DNA sequence that prevents it from being expressed. The cell looks normal; no red color.

2. **The Cre recombinase**: A second piece of genetic machinery is added: a bacterial protein called Cre, expressed only in the specific cell type you want to track. Cre recognizes the STOP sequence as a target and cuts it out. Once the STOP is removed, the red fluorescent gene is permanently activated.

3. **The heritable label**: Crucially, once Cre removes the STOP in a cell, that cell turns red *forever*. And when it divides, both daughter cells are also red. And their daughters. All descendants of that original labeled cell carry the red tag, even if the Cre gene itself is no longer active.

This means you can ask: "Every cell in this healed tendon that is red — was it, or was its ancestor, a tenocyte (Scx-expressing cell) before the injury?"

### Temporal control with CreERT2

A refinement called **CreERT2** adds a time dimension. The Cre protein is engineered to sit inactive in the cell's cytoplasm until you inject the mouse with a drug called tamoxifen. Tamoxifen releases the Cre, letting it enter the nucleus and activate the label.

This means you can precisely control *when* labeling happens — for example, label cells 2 weeks before injury to mark the pre-existing progenitor pool, or label cells during the healing phase to catch new arrivals. Without this control, you would label every cell that ever expressed your gene of interest, from embryonic development onward, which can be too broad.

### What fate mapping has revealed

Key findings from fate mapping in this research:

- After rotator cuff repair in adults, **Scx-lineage tenocytes virtually disappear** from the repair site by day 28 — they do not regenerate
- The repaired site is instead dominated by **SMA-lineage scar cells**
- In neonates, Scx-lineage tenocytes *do* migrate into defects and populate the repair — the defining difference between neonatal regeneration and adult scarring
- After muscle injury, Hic1-lineage mesenchymal progenitors give rise to new tenocytes and myotenocytes (cells at the muscle-tendon junction)

## 6. The Hic1 Discovery: A Universal Stamp on Stem Cells

The messiest problem in connective tissue biology is nomenclature: the same cell type has been called by at least a dozen different names depending on which lab isolated it, from which tissue, and when. "Mesenchymal stem cells," "bone marrow stromal cells," "FAPs," "fibro-adipogenic progenitors," "pericytes," "mural cells," "skeletal stem cells" — all describe overlapping populations that may, at their core, be the same thing.

A key contribution of this research program is proposing **Hic1** as a unifying molecular marker for all of these cells.

### What is Hic1?

**Hic1** (Hypermethylated in Cancer 1) is a transcription factor — a protein that controls whether other genes are switched on or off. In this context, Hic1 acts as a *quiescence enforcer*: it keeps progenitor cells in a dormant, non-dividing state. Cells that express Hic1 are MPs; when they differentiate into a specialized cell type (a tenocyte, a fat cell, a cartilage cell), they stop expressing Hic1.

This makes Hic1 useful as a cell identity marker: if a cell expresses Hic1, it is a progenitor. If it has lost Hic1, it has committed to a specialized fate.

### Hic1+ MPs across the body

Using the Hic1-CreERT2 tool (label all Hic1-expressing cells at the time of tamoxifen injection), this research has found Hic1+ MPs in **every vascularized tissue examined**: skeletal muscle, heart, skin, brain, liver, pancreas, bone, lung, fat. They always sit in perivascular locations — wrapped around blood vessels — suggesting blood vessels serve as the highway along which progenitors distribute throughout the body during development.

In the adult, these cells are uniformly quiescent. Over a year of observation in uninjured mice, their numbers do not change. They are waiting.

### What happens after injury

After injury to any of these tissues, Hic1+ MPs undergo the same stereotyped activation program:

1. **Cytokine response**: they detect the inflammatory signals released by damaged cells
2. **Proliferation**: they divide rapidly, expanding their numbers
3. **Provisional matrix**: they deposit fibronectin and collagen to scaffold the repair
4. **Resolution**: they return to quiescence as the tissue stabilizes

This same five-stage cycle has been documented in skin wounds, liver injury, heart attacks, bone fractures, stroke, muscle injury, and tendon repair. The master program appears to be conserved; only the tissue-specific details differ.

### The embryonic origin story

Where do Hic1+ MPs come from originally? Fate mapping at embryonic day 10.5 — just as the mouse limb bud is forming — shows that Hic1-expressing cells in the limb arise from the **sclerotome and syndetome** of the somites (segmented blocks of embryonic tissue along the spine), not from the neural crest (which gives rise to the face) or the lateral plate mesoderm (which gives rise to the limb skeleton's cartilage template). They migrate into the developing limb bud around embryonic day 11.5 and spend the next several days seeding every connective tissue compartment.

By adulthood, Hic1-lineage descendants include: tenocytes, myotenocytes, FAPs, pericytes, perineurial cells, fascia fibroblasts, white adipocytes, and bone marrow stromal cells. They are not just a tendon story — they are the origin story of almost all adult connective tissue cells.

## 7. The Tendon-Specific Story: Who Repairs What?

Having established the broad framework, here is how it plays out specifically in tendon:

### The patellar tendon punch model

The **patellar tendon punch injury** (a biopsy-punch instrument removes a 1.0 mm core from the tendon center) is the gold-standard model for studying adult tendon stem cell activity, because it is *regeneration-competent* in adults. This distinguishes it from full-thickness defects or Achilles tendon transection, which produce predominantly scar in adults.

Why does regeneration succeed in a punch injury but fail in other models? The likely answer is that the punch injury preserves the tendon sheath and the TSPCs within it. Full transection destroys the sheath, removing the very source of regenerative cells.

### The TSPC response

After punch injury, Tppp3+ cells in the paratenon sheath:
1. Receive a proliferation signal via **PDGFRα** (the PDGF receptor)
2. Divide and expand their numbers
3. Migrate into the defect
4. Differentiate into aligned FMOD+/TNC+ tenocyte-like cells
5. Deposit organized collagen (visible under specialized microscopy as a "crimp" pattern)

This entire sequence depends on **sensory nerve fibers** that re-grow into the injury site after damage. The nerves release **NGF (Nerve Growth Factor)**, which signals through the **TrkA receptor** on TSPCs. Block TrkA — either pharmacologically or by severing the nerve — and TSPCs fail to expand, and the tendon heals poorly. Treat with a TrkA-activating drug, and healing improves. This nerve-tendon-stem cell axis is an exciting therapeutic target.

### The TGFβ requirement

A second key signal in tendon regeneration is **TGFβ** (Transforming Growth Factor beta), particularly the TGFβ3 isoform. TGFβ3 acts as a **chemotactic signal** — a molecular compass that guides tenocytes to migrate toward the injury. Block TGFβ signaling pharmacologically, and tenocytes stop migrating into the defect, even though they proliferate normally. This is specifically a neonatal mechanism; adult tenocytes respond less vigorously to TGFβ3, contributing to their failure to repopulate wounds.

### The bone connection

An unexpected finding is how closely tendon biology connects to bone structure. The ridges, bumps, and protuberances on bone surfaces (called **bone superstructures**) — the greater tubercle of the humerus, the olecranon of the elbow, the radial tuberosity — are not part of the primary bone cartilage template. They are added later, in a "second wave" of bone morphogenesis, by a specialized subpopulation of Hic1+ MPs that co-express both **Scx** (the tendon marker) and **Sox9** (a cartilage marker). These tendon-cartilage hybrid progenitors build the anchoring structures where tendons meet bone.

Delete Hic1 from these progenitors in the embryo, and the long bones grow normally — but all the attachment site protuberances are missing. The bones look like featureless cylinders. The entheses (tendon-to-bone junctions) are disorganized. This proves that the same mesenchymal progenitors that build tendons also build the skeletal architecture that tendons attach to — they are a single integrated system.

After rotator cuff injury in adults, even when the tendon is surgically repaired, the bone at the enthesis permanently loses density and structure. Bone and tendon are mechanically coupled; when tendon fails, bone deteriorates too.

## 8. Reading Every Gene in Every Cell: Modern Sequencing Tools

Much of what we know about which cells are present and what they are doing comes from modern sequencing technologies. A brief primer:

### Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq)

Every cell in your body contains the same DNA, but each cell type selectively reads different portions of it — different genes are "on" or "off" in different cells. The set of genes currently active in a cell is its **transcriptome** (from "transcription," the process of reading DNA into RNA).

**Single-cell RNA-seq** measures the transcriptome of individual cells, one at a time, in tens of thousands of cells per experiment. Cells are broken apart gently, and each cell's RNA is tagged with a unique barcode, then sequenced. Bioinformatic analysis clusters cells with similar expression profiles together, revealing distinct populations.

The output is typically a **UMAP plot** — a 2D map where each dot is a cell, and similar cells cluster close together. Blobs of dots correspond to cell types (tenocytes, TSPCs, T-FAPs, immune cells, blood vessel cells, etc.). Genes that distinguish one blob from another are **marker genes** — molecular addresses for each cell type.

This is how the Tppp3+ TSPC and Tppp3− T-FAP populations were discovered: in a single-cell sequencing experiment on patellar tendon, these two populations appeared as distinct clusters with different marker genes, despite both living in the same anatomical niche.

### Single-cell ATAC-seq

While RNA-seq reads what genes are *on*, **ATAC-seq** asks which regions of the genome are *accessible* — "open" enough that the cell's molecular machinery could potentially turn them on. Think of it as the difference between a song currently playing (RNA-seq) and the full playlist of songs available on the device (ATAC-seq).

Together, RNA-seq and ATAC-seq reveal both what a cell is doing *right now* and what it is *poised to do* when conditions change. This is how researchers discovered that quiescent Hic1+ MPs have open chromatin at cell-cycle genes even while dormant — they are pre-loaded to proliferate the moment injury signals arrive.

### Spatial transcriptomics

Single-cell sequencing requires dissociating the tissue into individual cells, destroying the spatial relationships between them. You learn what each cell is, but not where it was. **Spatial transcriptomics** solves this by measuring gene expression while preserving the tissue's geography.

Two major platforms are used in this research:

- **Visium (10x Genomics)**: A tissue section is laid on a slide covered with tiny capture spots. RNA from the tissue diffuses down and binds to these spots, which encode spatial positions. After sequencing, each transcriptomic measurement is anchored to a physical location on the tissue. Resolution is roughly 5–55 µm — enough to distinguish anatomical regions.

- **Xenium (10x Genomics)**: Instead of collecting and sequencing RNA, fluorescent probes are hybridized directly to RNA molecules inside the intact tissue, and each transcript is imaged individually at subcellular resolution. This is a targeted approach (measures hundreds to thousands of pre-selected genes rather than all genes), but achieves true single-cell spatial resolution.

Spatial transcriptomics has been used in this field to show how sensory nerve denervation alters the molecular environment of injured tendon — not just which cells are present, but how the signaling "geography" of the wound changes.

## 9. The Bigger Picture: These Cells Are Everywhere

The tendon research sits within a much larger discovery: Hic1+ MPs appear to be the universal connective tissue progenitor across the entire body. Some highlights of their involvement in other systems:

**Heart**: Hic1+ cardiac FAPs (cFAPs) are the cellular source of the fibrofatty infiltrations that cause arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy — a heart disease that kills young athletes. Blocking PDGF signaling with nilotinib (a cancer drug) prevents cFAP differentiation into scar cells in mouse models.

**Skin**: Hic1+ dermal progenitors are the primary source of new hair follicles after large skin wounds. Hic1 itself suppresses this regeneration — deleting Hic1 in dermal progenitors *increases* hair follicle regeneration fourfold. This reveals Hic1 as a dial controlling the balance between scarring and regeneration.

**Brain**: Hic1+ perivascular progenitors in the brain (pericytes and perivascular fibroblasts) respond to ischemic stroke by migrating toward the lesion and either rebuilding blood vessels (pericytes) or laying down scar (perivascular fibroblasts). This is essentially the same FAP/pericyte split seen in peripheral tissues, playing out inside the brain after a stroke.

**Cancer**: A rare subset of Hic1+ MSCs (those co-expressing Pdgfra and Lgr5) is the cell-of-origin for **synovial sarcoma**, an aggressive cancer of young people. The cancer arises when a chromosomal translocation creates an abnormal fusion protein (SS18::SSX) that rewires the chromatin of these specific MSCs, causing them to behave like embryonic cells. This explains why synovial sarcomas arise at specific anatomical locations (joints, tendons) — because the susceptible cells live there.

The unifying message is: **one progenitor cell type, distributed throughout the body, serves as the universal first responder to tissue damage. Understanding — and controlling — its behavior is a master key for regenerative medicine across many diseases.**

## 10. Where Is This All Going?

The research program is moving toward translation: taking the basic biological discoveries and turning them into therapies.

### Restoring neonatal regenerative capacity

One of the most exciting findings is that transplanting neonatal Hic1+ MPs into injured adult tendons partially restores regenerative healing. This suggests that the *cell-intrinsic state* of the progenitor — the particular combination of genes active in a young progenitor versus an aged one — is at least as important as the tissue environment. If we can rejuvenate adult MPs to a neonatal-like state (perhaps by reprogramming specific gene networks), we might restore their regenerative potential.

### Targeting specific signals

The molecular pathways identified provide direct drug targets:
- **TrkA agonists**: A small-molecule drug that activates TrkA (mimicking the nerve-derived NGF signal) improved tendon healing in mouse experiments
- **TGFβ3 delivery**: Local delivery of TGFβ3 could promote tenocyte migration into repair sites
- **PDGF titration**: PDGF promotes both regeneration and fibrosis; separating these effects requires identifying which downstream pathways drive each outcome

### Biomaterial scaffolds

Understanding the biology informs scaffold design. A scaffold that:
- Provides aligned collagen fibers (to guide tenocyte orientation)
- Releases TGFβ3 locally (to drive tenocyte migration)
- Incorporates nerve-guidance structures (to support TrkA signaling from re-growing nerve fibers)
- Blocks FAP/T-FAP fibrotic differentiation

...would represent a rational, biology-driven approach to tendon repair — as opposed to the current practice of simply stitching the torn ends together and hoping for the best.

### Spatial transcriptomics as a diagnostic tool

Beyond basic research, spatial transcriptomics could eventually inform clinical decisions: is this healing tendon actually regenerating, or is it forming scar? Which molecular pathways are active? Is the nerve re-innervating properly? Biopsies with spatial transcriptomic readouts could, in the future, guide patient-specific treatment decisions.

## 11. Glossary of Key Terms

| Term | Plain-language definition |
|---|---|
| **Tenocyte** | The resident cell of mature tendon; produces and maintains the collagen matrix |
| **Scleraxis (Scx)** | A protein that marks tendon cells; used as a genetic "address label" to track them |
| **TSPC** | Tendon Stem/Progenitor Cell; a rare stem cell in the tendon sheath capable of generating new tenocytes after injury |
| **Tppp3** | A protein marker for TSPCs; expressed in the sheath, turned off as cells become tenocytes |
| **T-FAP** | Tendon Fibroadipogenic Progenitor; a sheath-resident cell that produces scar rather than tenocytes after injury |
| **FAP** | Fibroadipogenic Progenitor; a broader family of connective tissue progenitors capable of becoming fat cells or fibroblasts |
| **PDGFRα** | A receptor on the cell surface; responds to PDGF signals; marks both TSPCs and T-FAPs |
| **Fibroblast** | A cell whose primary job is producing extracellular matrix (collagen, fibronectin) |
| **Myofibroblast / αSMA+ cell** | An activated fibroblast; the primary driver of scar formation |
| **Mesenchymal Progenitor (MP)** | A multipotent stem-like cell that can become many connective tissue cell types |
| **Hic1** | A transcription factor that marks quiescent mesenchymal progenitors across the body |
| **Extracellular matrix (ECM)** | The protein meshwork outside cells that provides structural support (collagen, fibronectin, etc.) |
| **Lineage tracing** | An experimental method to permanently label a cell and track it and all its descendants over time |
| **Cre-lox** | The genetic system used for lineage tracing; Cre removes a STOP sequence to activate a fluorescent label |
| **CreERT2** | A tamoxifen-inducible version of Cre; allows labeling to be turned on at a chosen time |
| **scRNA-seq** | Single-cell RNA sequencing; measures which genes are active in each individual cell |
| **scATAC-seq** | Single-cell chromatin accessibility; measures which genomic regions are "open" and potentially active |
| **Spatial transcriptomics** | Measures gene expression while preserving the physical location of cells in tissue |
| **Visium** | A spatial transcriptomics platform that captures RNA at defined spots on a tissue slide |
| **Xenium** | A spatial transcriptomics platform that images individual RNA molecules in intact tissue |
| **UMAP** | A 2D visualization of single-cell data where similar cells cluster together |
| **Enthesis** | The attachment site where a tendon inserts into bone |
| **MTJ (myotendinous junction)** | The attachment site where a muscle connects to a tendon |
| **Bone superstructures** | Bumps, ridges, and protuberances on bone that anchor tendons; built by Hic1+ progenitors |
| **TGFβ** | Transforming Growth Factor beta; a signaling molecule that drives tenocyte migration |
| **NGF / TrkA** | Nerve Growth Factor and its receptor; the nerve-derived signal that activates tendon stem cells |
| **Fibrosis / Fibrotic repair** | Healing by scar (disorganized collagen) rather than by true tissue regeneration |
| **Quiescence** | A dormant, non-dividing cell state; how progenitors rest when not needed |
| **Transcription factor** | A protein that binds DNA and controls whether specific genes are switched on or off |
| **Conditional knockout** | An experiment where a gene is deleted only in a specific cell type using Cre-lox |
| **Paratenon** | The fibrous sheath surrounding certain tendons; the home of TSPCs |
